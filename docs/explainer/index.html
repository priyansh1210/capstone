Autonomous Model Maintenance

A supervisor that keeps a deployed bike-demand forecaster healthy on its own: it watches outcomes as labels arrive, decides when retraining is worth it, validates every replacement, and rolls back mistakes. This page explains how it works, why each part exists, how it was tested, and what the tests found.

  • 4 bike-share systems

The problem

A forecasting model starts to go stale the day it is deployed. Bike-share demand grows, collapses during a pandemic, shifts when e-bikes arrive, and moves with the weather and the calendar. Someone has to decide, every day, whether to keep the model, retrain it, and whether a retrained model really is better. This project hands that job to an autonomous supervisor and asks how it should make the decision.

Research question

Can selective, validated retraining keep prediction quality at the level of a fixed retraining schedule while avoiding unnecessary retraining?

Three things make the question hard in practice, and the system is built around all three:

  • Labels arrive late. Today's true demand is only known tomorrow, so the system always acts on yesterday's evidence.
  • Retraining is not automatically an improvement. A new model can be worse, so every replacement must pass a test on data it has never seen and survive a trial period.
  • "The error went up" is ambiguous. Error rises every winter, during blizzards and during a pandemic. A system that retrains on every rise wastes effort; one that ignores rises goes stale.

The answer, in short: classical drift detectors make poor retraining triggers on long real streams. The trigger proposed here, called probe, matches a well-tuned 28-day schedule with 40 % fewer full retraining cycles and does not retrain at all when nothing changes. A 14-day schedule is still 4-5 % more accurate than any trigger, so which policy to choose depends on what a retraining cycle costs. The cost section shows where each one pays off.

How the system works

The supervisor is a classic autonomic feedback loop, MAPE-K: Monitor, Analyse, Plan and Execute over shared Knowledge (Kephart & Chess, 2003). The deployed model is the managed element. The loop runs once per day, when that day's labels are revealed.

Loop stageWhat it does hereCode
MonitorScores the newly revealed day from predictions stored before the labels existed. Turns each hour's error into a seasonally adjusted signal and feeds it to a drift detector (ADWIN, Page-Hinkley or KSWIN).monitoring/monitor.py
AnalyseAsks whether action is justified: sustained deterioration against the model's reference (detector triggers), or a significantly better cheap challenger (probe). Also explains the evidence: input shift, demand bias, error level.policy/evidence.py, diagnostics.py
PlanChooses the validation window (strictly after everything the current model has seen) and the training windows, then fits six candidates: Ridge, Random Forest and gradient boosting, each on 365 and 730 days.policy/retrain.py
ExecutePromotes the best candidate only if it is at least 5 % better on the validation days, swaps it in atomically, runs 14 days of paired probation and rolls back automatically if it loses.policy/controller.py, probation.py
KnowledgeModel registry with every version's training and validation ranges and reference error; a SQLite store with every prediction and every decision with its reason, measurements and thresholds.models/registry.py, storage/store.py

Around the loop sit a deterministic replay simulator (it holds the truth and releases it one day at a time), an experiment runner, and a Streamlit dashboard with Overview, Decision timeline, Model versions and Experiments tabs for the live classroom demo.

One day in the loop

Most mistakes in evaluating a self-maintaining model are timing mistakes: a model validated on days it was trained on, or a decision made with a label that would not exist yet. The timeline below shows where every window sits on day t.

The rest of the boundary is enforced in code and covered by tests:

  • Information boundary. The controller receives a ControllerView that holds only observed covariates and revealed labels. The simulator pushes data in; the controller cannot reach back. Scenario truth (when a change was injected, its size) never reaches it (test_truth_hidden.py, test_leakage.py).
  • Predictions are scored once. Every hour's prediction is stored before its label exists and scored when the label is revealed. All policies are scored on exactly the same hours.
  • Weather from the past only. Predictions for hour h use the airport observation from h−1, forward-filled for at most 3 hours from earlier values.
  • Outages are not demand. Hours inside zero-trip runs of 6 hours or more (blizzards, hurricanes) are excluded from training and from the drift signal, but still scored.

What the detector triggers watch

Each revealed hour becomes one value: the absolute error on the log scale, capped at 3, divided by a monthly difficulty index.

  • Log scale: a 20 % miss at 1,000 trips counts the same as a 20 % miss at 100, which matters when demand triples over a decade.
  • Cap: one closed-system hour cannot dominate.
  • Monthly difficulty index: winter months are naturally harder (about 1.2-1.4x), so without it the detector "discovered" winter every December. The index is estimated once from the initial model's calibration errors.
  • Warm start: the detector starts with the errors the model was accepted on, so a model that is already worse on day one is noticed.

An alarm opens an investigation. The supervisor retrains only if the 14-day median of daily error exceeds the reference by 15 % (and 0.02 absolute) on three consecutive daily checks; otherwise the alarm is dismissed and logged with its numbers.

The probe trigger

A detector trigger compares the current model with its own past. The probe compares it with the best model that could be trained today, using a small, cheap fit as a stand-in.

Why this fixes the failures found in the first experiment:

  • No self-reference to inflate. Both models are scored on the same recent days, so a shock hurts both sides of the comparison equally.
  • Improvement is visible without degradation. When fresher data allows a better model, the challenger shows it even if the current model's error has not risen.
  • It rarely wastes a cycle. 96 % of the full cycles it requested were promoted, against 72 % for the 28-day schedule.

How the data were built and checked.

  • Counting: trips are counted by local start hour from every published archive, using a parser that handles five header styles and nested zip files.
  • Checks: 0 unparsed rows. Working-day demand peaks at 08:00 and 17:00 in every city-year, which confirms the timestamps are local.
  • Cross-check: the DC aggregation reproduces all 17,379 hours of the UCI dataset (totals within 1.1 %).
  • Provenance: raw files are checksummed (SHA-256), and both machines produced byte-identical inputs.
  • Inputs: calendar features and lagged weather only, never the target or its parts (member, casual and e-bike counts).

A data bug caught after the clean run

  • The symptom: New York's initial model showed an implausible error on its calibration year (MAE 2,451 trips/h, against 668 when replayed on 2019).
  • The cause: the Citi Bike 2018 archive stores every month twice, as a whole file and again as 1-million-row parts in month folders, plus an extra copy of April. The build had counted 36,404,221 rows for 17,548,339 trips.
  • The fix: the parser now drops a trip already seen in an earlier file of the same archive. It is verified identical on every other archive of all four systems. A new build check warns when a year's trips per day jump more than 1.6x above the neighbouring years: real growth never exceeded 1.21x, and the doubled year was 1.97x.
  • The re-run: only New York was re-run, with byte-identical policy code. The first results are archived, and the correction is disclosed in the report.

Keeping the test honest

A policy tuned on the data it is judged on proves little. The project went through three stages, and only the last counts as evidence.

  1. Development. Every threshold was chosen on DC 2011-2017 and on generated streams, with one fixed rule: lowest mean error relative to never retraining, with at most one retraining cycle on average when nothing changes; ties within 1 % go to fewer cycles. The chosen values were written to configs/frozen_policy.yaml.
  2. First benchmark, DC 2018-2024. This produced the diagnosis below. Because four design decisions were later informed by these years, they are reported as exploratory, and every one of those decisions is disclosed.
  3. Clean test. The code was frozen at git tag clean-test-v1 and run once on Chicago, New York, SF and DC 2025, data that no design decision had seen. Each city also ran with an injected abrupt change (demand x1.6 in commute hours). Generated streams (5 seeds) checked behaviour when nothing changes.

Statistics. Daily errors are autocorrelated (weather spells, seasons), so ordinary t-tests would be over-confident. Each pre-registered pair of policies is compared on the same days with:

  • a moving-block bootstrap 95 % confidence interval (28-day blocks, 2,000 resamples);
  • a Diebold-Mariano test with Newey-West variance (lag 7).

Two machines. A laptop (8 GB) wrote and tested the code. A 24-core desktop ran the heavy experiments. Git carried the code, the results and a shared memory/ folder between them. The laptop re-ran a desktop experiment and got identical numbers (MAE 114.67, 22 cycles).

What the numbers say

  1. Drift-detector triggers lose to the schedule on every held-out system, by 25-30 % on average. The diagnosis from DC repeated itself; see the next section.
  2. Probe matches the 28-day schedule (−0.1 %, no significant difference on any of 7 streams), with 40 % fewer full retraining cycles (271 vs 450) and 15-18 % less compute. On streams where nothing changes it never retrained (0 vs 26 cycles).
  3. No trigger beats retraining more often. A 14-day schedule was the most accurate policy on every clean stream: 4-5 % better than the 28-day schedule at 1.7x its compute.
  4. Timing a retrain to the change is not enough. The oracle, with three retrains at the known injected change, was worse than never retraining in all three cities. In Chicago and New York its retrains fell in February to May 2020 and locked in a COVID-era model. Even on generated streams it reached only 56.5, against 50.0 for eight untimed 91-day retrains. The value of retraining comes from repeatedly using fresh data.
  5. Ablations (each variant against its parent on the same four streams):
    • Seasonal difficulty index: essential. Without it the ADWIN trigger is 27 % worse, on all four systems.
    • Warm start: helped in New York (+14 % error without it) and nowhere else.
    • Probe acceleration: did nothing measurable.
    • The probe's significance test: costs 1.9 % accuracy but saves 19 % of cycles.
    • Probation: had no net effect on accuracy here. Its value is protection against faulty candidates, which the fault-injection tests exercise.

Why the drift detectors failed

The decision logs record every alarm with the numbers behind it, which made the failure mechanisms visible. There are two.

1. Reference inflation (DC, and again in Chicago)

Each model carries a reference: the error level it was accepted on. In Chicago, a model was validated on 23 March to 5 April 2020, the first weeks of the COVID-19 collapse, and promoted on 6 April. Its reference was rebased to 1.074, five times the initial model's 0.213. From then on the system judged every later error against a "normal" set during a crisis.

2. Blindness to an improvement that has become possible

In New York, SF and DC 2025 the detectors did not stall; they retrained less often than the schedule (New York: 30 to 56 cycles against 78) and ended 5-14 % worse. A deterioration trigger only acts when error rises above the model's own reference. After a change, the first new model is usually only a partial adaptation, and its error, compared with its own already-elevated reference, does not look worse. Fresher data could produce a much better model, but nothing in the trigger looks for it.

Lesson for autonomous systems

A health signal that the system defines relative to itself can be corrupted by the very event it is meant to detect. The probe replaces it with an external comparison made on the same days: the current model against a fresh challenger.

Which policy should an operator choose?

"Fewer cycles at equal error" only matters if a cycle costs something. In these experiments a cycle takes about 5 seconds, so the operator needs to know the break-even. The model below prices one full retraining cycle at κ days of the 28-day schedule's average forecast error and adds that to each policy's error:

cost(policy) = MAE / MAE(28-day schedule) + κ × cycles per day

Probes are charged at their measured compute share of a cycle (about 8 %). The analysis re-weights the executed runs; nothing is tuned, and κ is swept rather than chosen. Move the slider to set the price.

κ = 2.0 days

Cheapest on average: probe.

Bars: mean regret, the extra cost over the cheapest policy on each stream, in % of the 28-day schedule's error (bars capped at 25 %). Right column: on how many streams the policy is the cheapest.

  • Below about 1.4 days per cycle: retrain every 14 days.
  • From about 1.4 to 14 days: probe is the cheapest policy studied on long real streams (1.1 to 25 days if probes are free).
  • Above that: retrain rarely (every 56 or 91 days).
  • When nothing changes, never retraining or a detector trigger is cheapest. On a short stream with a single change, a rare schedule is hard to beat.

What is new:

  • an end-to-end comparison of retraining triggers under one shared, safe update procedure on long real streams with delayed labels;
  • a decision-log diagnosis of two failure modes, both seen again on held-out data;
  • a trigger that measures attainable improvement instead of watching degradation;
  • a cost analysis that sweeps the price of retraining instead of assuming it.

Limits

  • One task family. System-level hourly demand only; station-level demand and other domains are untested.
  • Thresholds from one city. Every threshold came from DC. The clean test measures how well they transfer, but four systems are a small sample of real histories.
  • Cheap retraining. A cycle costs seconds here; the cost model re-weights cheap runs rather than running expensive ones.
  • Injected change confounded with COVID-19. In Chicago and New York the injected change starts on 5 February 2020, weeks before the collapse, so only its paired cost is interpreted there.
  • Equivalence is not proven. "No significant difference" between probe and the 28-day schedule comes with intervals of about ±10 %.
  • Missing baselines. Continuously updated online learners and the cost-aware methods above were not run.
  • Observed weather. Weather comes from airport observations rather than forecasts.

Presentation questions

Questions an examiner on an autonomous-systems course is likely to ask, with short answers grounded in what was built and measured. Open one to see the answer.

Autonomy and the control loop

In what sense is your system autonomous, and how much autonomy does it have?

It closes the loop without a person. It senses outcomes (revealed labels), decides (keep, investigate, retrain), acts (trains, promotes, rolls back) and records why. Its autonomy is bounded: it can only choose among validated candidates, within a budget and a cooldown, and it cannot change its own thresholds. People set the policy and can inspect or override it, so it runs on the loop of a human supervisor, not under step-by-step control.

Map your system to the MAPE-K reference model.

Monitor: score the revealed day, seasonally adjusted error signal, drift detector. Analyse: deterioration evidence or the probe's paired test. Plan: validation window, training windows, six candidates. Execute: gate, atomic promotion, probation, rollback. Knowledge: model registry with references, prediction store and decision log.

What are the sensors and actuators of this autonomous system?

Sensors: the stream of stored predictions and their delayed labels, the covariates (calendar and weather), and timers for compute cost. Actuators: starting a training job, promoting or rolling back a model version. There is no physical actuation; the "plant" being controlled is the quality of a deployed model.

It is a feedback loop with delay. Could it oscillate, and how do you prevent that?

Yes in principle: retrain, roll back, retrain again. It is damped by hysteresis and rate limits: a 5 % margin to promote, a 5 % margin in two consecutive windows to roll back, a 14-day cooldown between cycles, and a budget. Rollback restores the old model's original reference, so a failed attempt does not distort later decisions.

What does the system do when it is uncertain?

It defaults to the status quo. Investigation needs three consecutive confirmations; the probe needs a significant paired test; the gate keeps the current model if no candidate is clearly better; when there are too few labels it waits and logs why. Uncertainty costs at most a delay, never an unvalidated change.

Why not let the model learn online, sample by sample?

Online updates are hard to validate, gate and roll back, and they absorb shocks such as outages or a pandemic directly into the model. Versioned, validated batch retraining keeps every model auditable and reversible. Online learners are a fair baseline that this project did not run; it is listed as future work.

Sensing and situational awareness

Labels arrive a day late. How can the system know the world has changed?

It cannot know immediately; it acts on yesterday's evidence. The detectors watch the error stream as labels arrive, and the probe compares models on the newest labelled days. Input shift in the weather covariates is also measured, but only to explain a decision, because input shift alone does not prove the model got worse.

How does it tell a real change from noise, a storm or a season?

Outage hours (6+ hours with zero trips) are excluded. A monthly difficulty index removes the normal seasonal rise. Deterioration must hold for a 14-day median on three consecutive days. The probe needs a statistically significant gain. The ablation shows the seasonal index matters: without it the ADWIN trigger was 27 % worse.

What is reference inflation, and why is it dangerous for any autonomous system?

The system's own idea of "normal" was reset during a crisis. A Chicago model accepted during COVID-19 got a reference five times the usual level, so 17 alarms in three years were judged harmless while its error doubled. Any self-referential health signal can be corrupted by the event it should detect. The fix is an external comparison: the probe tests the current model against a fresh challenger on the same days.

Does it detect concept drift or covariate shift?

It reacts to performance, which covers both when they matter. A covariate shift that does not hurt accuracy should not trigger retraining; a concept change with unchanged inputs must. The probe goes one step further: it acts when a better model is attainable, even if the current error has not risen.

Decision-making

Why is "can a better model be trained now?" a better question than "has my error worsened?"

The second question compares the model with itself, which fails in two measured ways: the reference can be inflated, and after a partial adaptation nothing looks worse even though fresher data would give a much better model. The first question compares two models on the same recent days, so shocks affect both sides and attainable improvement is visible directly.

Why a cheap challenger and not a full retrain every time?

A probe costs about 0.4 seconds against about 5 seconds for a full six-candidate cycle, and it predicts the gate well: 96 % of the cycles it requested were promoted, against 72 % for the 28-day schedule. It acts as a cheap first test before an expensive one.

How did you choose the thresholds? Isn't that just tuning until it works?

Every threshold was chosen on development data (DC 2011-2017 and generated streams) by a rule fixed in advance, written to a config file, and frozen with a git tag before the clean test on other cities. The evaluation stage that did inform design (DC 2018-2024) is reported as exploratory, with each such decision disclosed.

What does a wrong decision cost in each direction?

A needless retrain costs compute and risks a worse model, which the gate and probation contain. A missed retrain costs forecast error for as long as the stale model serves: three years in Chicago for the ADWIN trigger. The cost model puts both on one scale and shows the break-even price.

So which policy should an operator actually use?

It depends on what a retraining cycle costs, measured in days of forecast error. Below about 1.4 days, retrain every 14 days. Between about 1.4 and 14 days, probe was the cheapest policy on the real streams. Above that, retrain rarely. If the system rarely changes, never retraining or a detector trigger is cheapest.

Why not learn the retraining policy with reinforcement learning?

There are only a few hundred decisions per stream, rewards arrive late and noisily, and the environment is itself drifting, so an RL agent would need a simulator and far more episodes than history provides. It would also be harder to bound and audit. Regol et al. (ICML 2025) make a similar argument against offline RL for this problem.

Safety and fail-safes

What prevents the system from deploying a bad model?

Three layers. A validation gate on 14 days no model has seen (≥ 5 % better than the current one). Atomic promotion, so a crash leaves the old model serving. Fourteen days of paired probation with automatic rollback. Fault-injection tests force weak candidates and a weak activation and check that the system rejects or rolls them back.

What happens if training fails halfway?

The current model keeps serving and the failure is logged with its error. A failed artifact save also aborts the promotion. Both cases are tested (test_failures.py).

Why compare paired predictions in probation instead of just watching the new model's error?

Because the world can change during probation. If a storm or a holiday raises everyone's error, an unpaired check would roll back a good model. Comparing old and new on the same hours cancels shared shocks; a test checks exactly this case.

What are the hard bounds on the system's behaviour?

A cycle budget, a cooldown, one training job at a time, promotion only of validated candidates, no access to future data, and no ability to edit its own thresholds or policy. Everything it does is logged with the measurements and thresholds that justified it.

Could bad input data make the system misbehave?

Yes, and it happened. New York's 2018 archive stored every month twice, which inflated the calibration-year reference and helped stall the detector there. The anomaly was visible in the logs (calibration error 2,451 against 668 the next year). The parser now removes repeated trips, and the build warns when a year's trips per day jump implausibly. Input validation is part of an autonomous system's safety case, not an afterthought.

What would you change before using this in a safety-critical domain?

Human approval for promotions; gates per segment (peak hours, rare conditions), not just on average error; staged roll-outs; stronger input monitoring; formal runtime monitors for the state machine; and explicit operating limits outside which the system hands control back to people.

Human oversight and accountability

How can a person find out why the system did something?

Every action is logged with its reason, the measurements and the thresholds, for example "deterioration confirmed: error 0.288 vs reference 0.205 (+41 %); model under-predicts total demand by 24 %; no large input shift". The dashboard shows the decision timeline and every model version with its training and validation ranges.

Where is the human in the loop?

Humans set the frozen policy and budget, can request a retraining (it still passes the gate), review the logs and pause a run from the dashboard. Test-only fault injections are clearly labelled in the dashboard. The design keeps humans on the loop with full visibility instead of approving each step.

If the system makes a bad call, who is responsible?

The organisation that deploys it and sets its policy. The system's job is to make every decision traceable: what it saw, which rule applied, and what it changed. Its explanations separate input shift, demand bias and error level, and none claims to prove a cause.

Verification and evaluation

How do you test a system whose behaviour unfolds over years?

With a deterministic replay simulator that releases data one day at a time with delayed labels, so years run in minutes. On top of that: 60 automated tests (leakage, label delay, gates, rollback, failures, isolation between policies, persistence and resume, the parser and the statistics), fault injection, a headless demo rehearsal, and identical results on two machines.

How do you know no future information leaks into decisions?

The controller only receives a view with observed covariates and revealed labels; the simulator holds everything else. Validation windows start after the current model's training and selection end. Tests check that the controller holds no reference to the simulator or the scenario, never imports scenario code, and makes identical decisions when the hidden scenario description is changed. Further tests check that leaky columns are excluded and that weather is lagged and never back-filled.

Why test on other cities?

An autonomous policy must work in operating environments it was not designed in. Chicago, New York and SF have different sizes, climates and histories; DC 2025 is new time in the same place. All thresholds came from DC development data, so the clean test measures transfer.

You found a bug after the frozen run. Doesn't that invalidate the clean test?

It was a data bug, not a change to the method. The parser was fixed and verified identical on every other archive, and only New York was re-run, with byte-identical policy code. The first results are kept and the correction is disclosed. The overall conclusions held: detectors still lost on every system, and probe still matched the schedule.

Is the oracle an upper bound on performance?

No. Retraining three times right after a known change was worse than never retraining in all three cities, because those retrains landed in the COVID-19 collapse and locked in that model. Even on generated streams eight untimed retrains beat three perfectly timed ones. Retraining pays off by repeatedly using fresh data.

Why block bootstrap and Diebold-Mariano instead of a normal t-test?

Daily errors are strongly autocorrelated (weather spells, seasons, regime changes). Treating 2,000 days as independent would overstate confidence. Resampling 28-day blocks and using a Newey-West variance account for that dependence.

Limits and transfer

Your method only ties the schedule. Why is that a useful result?

Probe is the only policy that both matched the schedule on drifting real data and did not retrain when nothing changed (0 vs 26 cycles). It needs 40 % fewer full cycles, and its requests pass the gate 96 % of the time. When retraining is expensive or needs sign-off, that is the saving that matters; when it is cheap, the cost model says to retrain often, and the report says so.

Would this work for the perception model of a self-driving car?

The structure transfers: shadow evaluation, gates, paired probation, rollback, an external comparison instead of a self-referential alarm, and an explicit price for retraining. The setting differs. Labels are expensive and slow, retraining is costly (the regime where probe-like triggers or rare retraining win), and safety requires per-scenario validation, certification and human approval before promotion.

What if the world stops changing?

Probe keeps probing every 14 days but starts no cycles: 0 cycles in all five stationary test streams, at a cost of about 34 seconds of probing over two years. A schedule would have retrained 26 times for no gain.

What would make it fully autonomous?

It already runs unattended. What remains with people is deliberate: the price of a retraining cycle, the policy thresholds and the operating domain. Letting the system tune its own thresholds online is possible but risks drift in the decision rules themselves, so it would need its own safeguards.

What is the biggest limitation, and what would you do next?

One task family with cheap retraining. Next: run the cost-aware methods (Cara, Regol et al.) and online learners as baselines, add a non-bike domain, test a setting where retraining is genuinely expensive, and check sensitivity to label delay and gate margins.
